PiBoy DMG 64-Bit Port

Getting a PiBoy DMG onto 64-bit Raspberry Pi OS Trixie after Experimental Pi shut down — display, audio, controller driver, fan control and charging, rebuilt from Experimental Pi's own settings — then migrated onto the card the handheld shipped with, and the game library and controls put back in order.

Hardware Pi 4B rev 1.4, 8 GB Target Trixie arm64, kernel 6.18 From RetroPie 4.8.12 / Buster, 32-bit Verified 22 Aug 2026

Experimental Pi is gone, and its last official image — PiBoy DMG 1.0.7, June 2024 — is 32-bit RetroPie on an end-of-life Debian. There is no supported 64-bit path, and RetroPie itself has never shipped an official aarch64 image. Everything below was worked out by hand from Experimental Pi's own config.txt and the GPL driver source left behind on the original card.

It works. The panel, audio, controls, battery telemetry and fan all come up from cold with no HDMI attached and no intervention.

Eleven traps that cost real time

Each of these presents as a hardware fault. None of them is one.

Nothing answers the power switch, so every shutdown is unclean

The switch does not cut the rail directly. It tells the XPi, which asserts a shutdown request and then waits before killing power. The driver already decodes it — stat_val = data[5]&0xC6; //VBus,Shutdown,VSTAT2,VSTAT1 — but nothing acts on it, and no gpio-shutdown overlay exists (Experimental Pi's config had none either; their closed osd binary was the listener).

Measured on this hardware: the bit is 0x40, active-low, and there are ~31 seconds between the request and the rail dropping.

05:02:14  XPi status 198 -> 134   (0xC6 -> 0x86)   switch OFF
05:02:45  ...log simply stops...                    power cut
shutdown markers in that boot: 0

Until something listens, every power-off corrupts a live filesystem — and a nudged or marginal switch looks exactly like a spontaneous power-off: full battery, normal temperature, no throttling flags.

Do not solve this in a daemon. Polling the byte from userspace works, but only while that process is alive and scheduled — which it is not during early boot, in the restart window after a crash, after systemctl stop, or during a normal reboot when it is killed while the rail is still up. Report KEY_POWER from the driver instead and the whole problem moves into the kernel; see the build steps.

Raspberry Pi OS silently forces volatile journald

/usr/lib/systemd/journald.conf.d/40-rpi-volatile-storage.conf sets Storage=volatile to spare the SD card, and drop-ins beat journald.conf. So creating /var/log/journal and setting Storage=persistent in the main file appears to work and does nothing — journald even reports success while still writing to /run. Every crash keeps vanishing, and journalctl -b -1 stays empty.

Override with a later-sorting drop-in in /etc, and cap the size since you are re-enabling flash writes Experimental Pi deliberately disabled:

# /etc/systemd/journald.conf.d/50-persistent.conf
[Journal]
Storage=persistent
SystemMaxUse=64M

Verify with journalctl --header | grep "File path" — it must say /var/log/journal, not /run/log/journal. Do this before you need it.

The board runs permanently under-voltage — and Experimental Pi hid it

The XPi's boost converter cannot hold the 4.63 V the Pi's under-voltage comparator expects; that detector is calibrated for a wall supply, not a battery handheld. Experimental Pi's answer was avoid_warnings=2, which does not merely suppress the warning icon — it disables the firmware's throttling response. Omit it and the firmware parks the SoC at its 600 MHz floor and never releases it.

Measured on a stock 1800 MHz config: 6436 ms clamped versus 2295 ms with the setting restored — 2.8× — and throttled goes from 0x50005 to a clean 0x0. It presents perfectly as a bad PSU or cable. It is neither, and no supply will fix it.

An open firmware issue reports the setting failing on Bookworm. It works on Trixie. Understand what you are accepting: the rail genuinely does sag, and you are choosing to ride through the dips rather than back off — so be scrupulous about clean shutdowns.

scaling_cur_freq reports a frequency you are not getting

While clamped, /sys/…/scaling_cur_freq cheerfully read 1800 MHz — it reports what the kernel requested, not what the firmware delivered. vcgencmd measure_clock arm read 600 MHz and was correct. Pinning the governor to performance changed nothing.

Trust vcgencmd, or better, trust a stopwatch: time a fixed workload at powersave and at performance. If the two are within a few percent, the firmware is ignoring both and something else is in charge.

config.txt silently truncates lines at 98 characters

No warning, no log entry. A long dtoverlay= line simply loses its tail. Ours was 162 characters, so vactive=480 arrived as vactive=4 and the panel ran at 640×4 — a lit backlight showing essentially nothing. The dropped rgb666-padhi then caused trap three.

Split long overlays across dtparam= lines, which bind to the preceding dtoverlay=. Verify what actually landed by reading the live device tree, never by reading config.txt back.

Trixie dropped custom.toml for cloud-init

The Bookworm-era custom.toml is read by nothing on Trixie. First boot runs, resizes the root filesystem, rewrites cmdline.txt — and ignores your provisioning entirely, leaving no user account and no Wi-Fi. The datasource is now NoCloud seeding from /boot/firmware.

Confusingly, the ssh marker file still works, because sshswitch is a separate older mechanism. Partial success makes this one hard to read.

DPI and PWM audio both want GPIO 18

The default DPI pinctrl is the contiguous 18-bit group, GPIO 0–21, which includes pin 18. Add audremap,pins_18_19 and the two collide — vc4_dpi fails to claim the pin, tears itself down, and takes all of KMS with it. You lose the panel, HDMI and HDMI audio at once, which looks nothing like a pin conflict.

The rgb666-padhi layout exists precisely to avoid this: it skips 10, 11, 18 and 19, leaving 18/19 free for audio. That is why Experimental Pi chose it.

A kernel update silently orphans the module — and never rmmod it

Modules install under /lib/modules/$(uname -r)/. An apt upgrade that pulls a new kernel (ours went 6.18.34 → 6.18.39 mid-session) leaves the module behind at the old path, so after the next boot there is no controller, no telemetry and no fan control, silently. Rebuild against the running kernel, or better, register it with DKMS so upgrades rebuild it for you — which is exactly what Experimental Pi did.

Separately: do not rmmod xpi_gamecon on a running device. The XPi microcontroller is what supplies power to the Pi, and the driver talks to it at 125 Hz. Stop that conversation and the board appears to conclude the host is gone — ours powered off mid-command. Install the new .ko and reboot instead.

ALSA sends audio to HDMI by default

The PWM card is enumerated third — card 0: vc4hdmi0, card 1: vc4hdmi1, card 2: Headphones [bcm2835] — and with no asound.conf, ALSA picks card 0. Everything plays silently out the HDMI port while the speaker sits idle. The mixer is unmuted and correct; nothing looks wrong anywhere.

Write /etc/asound.conf pointing at the card by name, never by index — plugging or unplugging an HDMI display renumbers the cards and an index-based config breaks without warning.

The volume control's percentage scale is 106 dB wide

Mapping the hardware wheel 1:1 onto amixer percentage feels broken — audible only above roughly 30% of travel. The control spans −102.39 dB to +4.00 dB and percentage is linear in dB, so 30% lands at −70 dB. Measured on this hardware:

 30% -> -70.47dB     60% -> -38.56dB
 80% -> -17.28dB    100% -> +4.00dB

Map the wheel onto the usable top of the range instead (0 = true mute, 1–100 → ~55–100%). Both the wheel and the driver report correctly throughout; it is only the mapping between them that has to be built.

The first-run wizard renames uid 1000

If provisioning fails and the setup wizard runs, it renames the stock pi account to whatever username you pick and moves its home directory. Anything staged in /home/pi travels with it — which is worth knowing both as a hazard and as a trick: an authorized_keys written to /home/pi before first boot survives into the new user's home and gives you SSH regardless of what the wizard did.

Sequence

The build

Steps are numbered because the order matters — in particular, do not run anything heavy before step 6, since nothing controls the fan until then.

  1. Flash Raspberry Pi OS Lite, 64-bit

    Lite rather than Desktop: fewer moving parts, and the panel test happens on a plain console. Verify the write by hashing the device, not by trusting dd — its progress figure reports the page cache, and conv=fsync only flushes at the end.

    xz -dc raspios-trixie-arm64-lite.img.xz \
  | sudo dd of=/dev/sdX bs=4M conv=fsync status=progress

sudo sh -c 'sync; echo 3 > /proc/sys/vm/drop_caches'   # else you hash RAM
sudo head -c $(xz -l --robot *.img.xz | awk '/^file/{print $5}') /dev/sdX \
  | sha256sum

  2. Provision first boot with cloud-init

    Three files at the root of the boot partition. Without them you get the setup wizard, which needs a keyboard and a working screen — the very thing you are trying to test.

    # meta-data — a NEW instance-id forces modules to re-run
instance-id: piboy64-001
local-hostname: piboy64

# network-config — netplan v2
version: 2
wifis:
  wlan0:
    dhcp4: true
    optional: true
    access-points:
      "YOUR_SSID":
        password: "YOUR_PSK"

    Belt and braces: also write your public key straight to /home/pi/.ssh/authorized_keys on the root filesystem (mode 700/600, owned by uid 1000). It costs nothing and survives the wizard.

  3. Enable the DPI panel

    Split across short lines — see trap one. Keep HDMI enabled while working; a second display is what lets you debug the first.

    dtoverlay=vc4-kms-dpi-generic
dtparam=clock-frequency=32000000
dtparam=hactive=640,hfp=80,hsync=80,hbp=80
dtparam=vactive=480,vfp=13,vsync=13,vbp=13
dtparam=rgb666-padhi
dtparam=width-mm=71,height-mm=53

  4. Restore PWM audio

    Without this the amplifier's input floats and the speaker emits a steady high-frequency squeal. Harmless, but unmistakable.

    audio_pwm_mode=2
dtoverlay=audremap,pins_18_19

  5. Restore the clock

    One line, and the difference between a 600 MHz handheld and an 1800 MHz one. See trap one for what it actually does and what you are trading away.

    avoid_warnings=2

    Verify by timing real work rather than reading a clock value:

    vcgencmd get_throttled           # want 0x0
time dd if=/dev/zero bs=1M count=300 2>/dev/null | sha256sum
# ~2300 ms free · ~6400 ms clamped

  6. Build the controller driver

    The GPL source lives at /usr/src/xpi_gamecon-1.0 on the original card — 556 lines, bit-banging a two-wire protocol to the XPi microcontroller on GPIO 26/27. Three edits make it build on 6.18. Experimental Pi's Makefile also has KVERSION := 'uname -r' in single quotes, which expands to a literal string; use $(shell uname -r).

    #include <linux/of.h>          // of_device.h no longer pulls this in
#include <linux/version.h>

#if LINUX_VERSION_CODE >= KERNEL_VERSION(6,16,0)
#define GC_FROM_TIMER(p,t,m)  timer_container_of(p,t,m)
#else
#define GC_FROM_TIMER(p,t,m)  from_timer(p,t,m)
#endif

#if LINUX_VERSION_CODE >= KERNEL_VERSION(6,15,0)
#define GC_DEL_TIMER_SYNC(t)  timer_delete_sync(t)
#else
#define GC_DEL_TIMER_SYNC(t)  del_timer_sync(t)
#endif

    Install it where it survives reboots:

    sudo cp xpi_gamecon.ko /lib/modules/$(uname -r)/extra/
sudo depmod -a
echo xpi_gamecon | sudo tee /etc/modules-load.d/xpi_gamecon.conf

  7. Replace the fan controller

    Experimental Pi's osd binary drove the fan curve, and it cannot run on 64-bit — see the closing section. Until something replaces it the fan is unmanaged, so do this before any sustained load. The daemon below reproduces Experimental Pi's osd.cfg bands and adds a full-speed band above 78 °C that the original lacked.

    duty_for() {   # millidegrees -> 0..255 PWM
    local t=$1
    if   [ "$t" -ge 78000 ]; then echo 255   # 100%  (added)
    elif [ "$t" -ge 75000 ]; then echo 191   #  75%
    elif [ "$t" -ge 70000 ]; then echo 128   #  50%
    elif [ "$t" -ge 65000 ]; then echo 102   #  40%
    elif [ "$t" -ge 60000 ]; then echo  77   #  30%
    elif [ "$t" -ge 55000 ]; then echo  51   #  20%
    elif [ "$t" -ge 50000 ]; then echo  26   #  10%
    else                          echo   0
    fi
}

    Give the fan downward hysteresis or it will flap. Measured idle temperature swing on this hardware is ~2400 millidegrees, so anything less than that oscillates across a band edge every sense cycle — writing sysfs and logging each time. 3500 settles it: one transition a minute instead of one every five seconds.

    Run it from a systemd unit with Restart=always and let the script exit non-zero when the sysfs tree is missing. Do not use ConditionPathExists — a failed condition makes systemd skip the unit, and Restart never applies to something that was never started, so a boot race silently leaves you with no fan control.

  8. Report the power switch as KEY_POWER

    Do this before you rely on the device at all. Emit the switch as a key event from gc_timer() on the edge, on a device carrying only KEY_POWER:

    bool off = !(stat_val & 0x40);
if (off != last) {
        last = off;
        input_report_key(pwr_dev, KEY_POWER, off);
        input_sync(pwr_dev);
        if (off) mod_timer(&backstop, jiffies + 8 * HZ);
}

    udev tags any KEY_POWER carrier with power-switch, and systemd-logind's HandlePowerKey then runs a full inhibitor-aware shutdown — no policy code of your own. Verify with udevadm info -q all -p /class/input/eventN (the tag lands on the event node, not its parent) and look for logind's "Watching system buttons on …" line.

    Keep it off the gamepad device: one advertising both joystick axes and KEY_POWER gets enumerated by SDL and shows up as a phantom button in emulators.

    Add a kernel backstop too, since this is safety-critical and logind can be absent or wedged: arm a timer on the press and call orderly_poweroff(false) if system_state is still SYSTEM_RUNNING when it fires. Re-check the bit inside it, so a switch flipped back on lets it expire harmlessly.

  9. Route audio to the speaker

    Getting sound out of GPIO 18/19 is not enough — ALSA still has to be told to use that card. Reference it by name so an HDMI display cannot renumber it out from under you.

    # /etc/asound.conf
pcm.!default { type plug; slave.pcm "hw:Headphones,0" }
ctl.!default { type hw;   card Headphones }

    RetroArch opens its audio device at launch, so restart any running emulator after changing this.

  10. Expose the battery properly

    Do not reimplement Experimental Pi's overlay. Register the pack as a standard power_supply device in the driver and EmulationStation, RetroArch and every other tool pick it up natively — no drawing code, no DRM planes. Roughly 80 lines, backed by values the driver already polls.

    Guard the startup window: percent_val is 0 until the first XPi packet arrives, and advertising an empty battery — even briefly — invites an emergency shutdown on any system running battery policy. Gate on the driver's lastgood counter and report UNKNOWN until real data exists.

  11. Install RetroPie

    RetroPie's own detection accepts this platform (platform=rpi4, flags 64bit gl vulkan x11, error=none) but sets has_binaries=0 — prebuilt packages exist only for Debian 12 and 32-bit Debian 10/11. On Trixie everything compiles from source. Budget accordingly: RetroArch plus three cores plus EmulationStation took a few hours on a Pi 4.

    git clone --depth=1 https://github.com/RetroPie/RetroPie-Setup.git
sudo ./retropie_packages.sh retroarch
sudo ./retropie_packages.sh lr-nestopia lr-snes9x lr-mgba
sudo ./retropie_packages.sh emulationstation
sudo ./retropie_packages.sh runcommand
sudo ./retropie_packages.sh autostart enable

    Run it detached under nohup setsid — a multi-hour build should not die with your SSH session. Then set governor = "performance" in runcommand.cfg so games get the full 1800 MHz.

  12. Reboot and verify cold

    Nothing counts until it survives a power cycle with no monitor attached. Check the module autoloaded, js0 exists, the daemon is active, DPI-1 reports 640x480, and /sys/class/power_supply/xpi-battery exists.

Sequence

Moving to the real card

The build above happens on a small spare card, which is the right way to do it. But the finished system has to end up on the card the handheld shipped with — and on this hardware that card still holds the only copy of Experimental Pi's image. Everything below is shaped by that: the moment you format the target, the original exists nowhere else.

Image the original first, and verify the image

Use partclone, not dd. It walks the filesystem and copies allocated blocks only, so a 939 GB partition holding 35 GB of data yields a 23 GB archive instead of a 939 GB one. Pipe it straight into zstd.

partclone.ext4 -c -s /dev/mmcblk0p2 -o - | zstd > root.pcl.zstd
sfdisk --dump /dev/mmcblk0 > partition-table.sfdisk
sha256sum *.pcl.zstd partition-table.sfdisk > MANIFEST.txt
A compressor turns zero input into a valid archive. If the capture produces nothing, zstd still emits a well-formed 13-byte empty frame and exits 0 — a success by every check except size. Assert a floor of a megabyte or so, write to .part and rename only on proven success, and check PIPESTATUS for both ends of the pipe rather than the pipeline's exit code. Note also that partclone.dd takes no -c flag; for a FAT boot partition use partclone.vfat, which walks the FAT properly.

Verify the checksums before the wipe, not after. This is the last gate in front of a one-way door, and a truncated archive passes a file-size glance and then fails to decompress hours later, long after the source is gone.

Replay the captured table — do not invent a layout

The obvious move is to write a fresh partition table sized for the new card. It is also the one that quietly destroys the rollback. A hand-rolled layout with a different start sector made p2 263 MiB smaller than the original, and partclone refuses to restore into a smaller target — which would have rendered the 23 GB image unrestorable to the very card it came from, discovered only after that card had been formatted.

sfdisk /dev/mmcblk0 < partition-table.sfdisk

Replaying the dump verbatim also preserves the MBR label-id, so PARTUUIDs keep meaning what they meant and a future restore of Experimental Pi image needs no hand-editing.

findmnt <a> <b> is not "check these two devices." The two-argument form means source and target, so a guard written that way silently passes while checking something you did not mean. Use --source and --target explicitly. Worth stating plainly because this bug was written twice in one night, in two different scripts, both times inside a safety check.

Copy at file level — and measure the link before blaming rsync

A file-level rsync beats a block clone here: only the real data moves, and the target filesystem is already sized for the new card. Two passes, because a FAT boot partition cannot hold ownership or permissions and a single -aHAX run over it produces a flood of errors that buries real ones.

rsync -aHAX --numeric-ids --info=progress2 \
  -e "ssh -i KEY" --rsync-path="sudo rsync" \
  --exclude='/proc/*' --exclude='/sys/*' --exclude='/dev/*' \
  --exclude='/run/*' --exclude='/tmp/*' --exclude='/swapfile' \
  --exclude='/var/swap' --exclude='/boot/firmware/*' \
  pi@HOST:/ /mnt/tgt/

rsync -rlt -e "ssh -i KEY" --rsync-path="sudo rsync" \
  pi@HOST:/boot/firmware/ /mnt/tgt/boot/firmware/   # no perms on FAT
Wi-Fi to Wi-Fi through an access point crosses the air twice. This transfer first ran at 133 kB/s, projecting a twelve-hour copy of 5.7 GB. The instinct is to blame latency and per-file round-trips — but a measurement showed RTT was a healthy 35 ms, and xfr#15 said only fifteen large files had moved, so it was raw bandwidth all along. Both machines were wireless clients, so every packet traversed the radio twice and both ends were weak-signal. Putting one end on ethernet took it to 4.8–7.1 MB/s, a 36–53× improvement, and dropped RTT to 9.8 ms. Measure throughput and latency separately before choosing a fix; they point at different problems.

Expect the copied size to be smaller than the source and do not panic at it. Here df read 6.07 GB on the Pi against 3.94 GB on the card — entirely the deliberately-excluded 2 GB /var/swap.

Rewrite the identifiers by mountpoint, not by string substitution

rsync copies /etc/fstab and cmdline.txt verbatim, so they still name the build card's PARTUUIDs. Left alone the Pi drops to an initramfs prompt: firmware finds the kernel fine, because it reads the FAT partition it booted from by position, and then the kernel goes looking for a root device that is not in this machine.

The tempting fix is s/old/new/. Don't — it cannot tell the boot entry from the root entry, so if the two are ever ordered differently it maps them backwards and still reports success. Match on the mountpoint instead:

P1=$(blkid -s PARTUUID -o value /dev/mmcblk0p1)
P2=$(blkid -s PARTUUID -o value /dev/mmcblk0p2)

awk -v p1="$P1" -v p2="$P2" '
  /^[[:space:]]*#/ || NF < 2 { print; next }
  $2 == "/"              { $1 = "PARTUUID=" p2 }
  $2 == "/boot/firmware" { $1 = "PARTUUID=" p1 }
  { print }' /mnt/tgt/etc/fstab

sed -i -E "s#root=PARTUUID=[0-9a-fA-F-]+#root=PARTUUID=${P2}#" \
  /mnt/tgt/boot/firmware/cmdline.txt
Two checks worth automating here. cmdline.txt must stay a single line — the firmware reads the first line only, and a stray newline silently discards every parameter after it. And re-run the 98-character audit on config.txt after the copy, since a truncated overlay line is a black screen with no other symptom.

Let rsync verify itself

Counting files by hand does not settle whether the copy is complete: an unprivileged scan cannot descend into root-only directories, so the totals never reconcile — 104,564 against 104,542 here, with 21 unreadable directories, which proves nothing in either direction. Re-run the identical transfer with -n --itemize-changes and let rsync answer.

An empty result is the wrong expectation. The source is a running system; logs, journals and caches move on while you copy. This run reported twelve differences and every one was traceable to the ethernet cable being plugged in mid-copy — a new DHCP lease, a regenerated NetworkManager key, resolv.conf with identical content and a newer mtime. Filter the known-volatile paths and review what is left, rather than chasing zero.

One consequence of that regenerated key is worth predicting: /var/lib/NetworkManager/secret_key did not exist at copy time, so NetworkManager rebuilds it on first boot, the DHCP identity changes, and the machine comes back on a different IP. Budget for finding it rather than assuming the old address.

Restore bulk data locally, never across the network

ROMs are the largest thing on the card and the least suited to a network copy. Put the backup medium in the handheld and copy disk-to-disk: 26 GB over USB is minutes, the same 26 GB over a shared Wi-Fi link is most of a day.

Do not extract as root. EmulationStation runs as the console user; ROMs owned by root are readable but it can never write a gamelist or scrape artwork. Extract as the ordinary user and have the script refuse if invoked under sudo — use sudo only for mounting the backup medium, read-only. Restore ROMs and BIOS only: Experimental Pi's retropie-configs and dotfiles name 32-bit emulator binaries and paths that do not exist in this build, and restoring them wholesale replaces a working configuration with one pointing at nothing.

Finally, a system copied this way shares its machine-id with the card it came from. Keep the build card as a rollback — it is a complete working system and a thirty-second recovery — but never let both boot onto the network at once, or they will contest the same DHCP identity.

What the finished migration looks like. / at 938 G on PARTUUID=e22bcd10-02, 2000 MHz under load, throttled=0x0 at 1.01 V and 72.5 °C, driver loaded, both daemons active. Confirm the root PARTUUID the kernel actually used — not the one you wrote — since that is the only proof the rewrite took.
Afterwards

Putting the library back

Restoring 26 GB of roms is the easy part — plug the backup medium into the handheld and copy disk-to-disk, never across the network. What took longer was discovering that the library itself had been wrong for years, in ways nothing ever reported.

EmulationStation matches by extension, and says so only in its log

A system whose directory holds files it does not recognise is not an error — it is silence. The only trace is one line in es_log.txt:

System "segacd" has no games! Ignoring it.

On this device roms/segacd held 98 .md files: Genesis roms, not Sega CD disc images. Sega CD accepts only .iso .cue .chd .m3u, so every one of them was invisible. The same 98 roms also sat in roms/sega32x, which does accept .md — so the collection appeared, mislabelled as 32X — while roms/megadrive was empty. There were no 32X or Sega CD games on the device at all.

Read that log after every library change. Grep it for has no games and for is not a directory. Both are silent in the UI, and between them they catch misfiled roms, wrong extensions and missing directories — the three ways a library breaks without complaining.

Find duplicates by size first, then hash

Two files of different sizes cannot be identical, so hashing an entire 26 GB library to find duplicates reads about twelve times more than necessary. Inventory sizes, keep only the sizes that occur more than once, and hash that subset — here 2,168 files out of 6,671, which found 212 distinct files existing in more than one copy and 3.2 GB of redundancy.

find "$R" -type f -printf '%s\t%p\n' | sort -n > inventory
awk -F'\t' 'NR==FNR{c[$1]++;next} c[$1]>1' inventory inventory | cut -f2-
Identical bytes do not always mean redundant files. Of 212 duplicate groups, 34 were not roms at all. MAME keeps a per-game .cfg, and two games that have never been reconfigured hold byte-identical defaults — deleting ARKNOIDU.CFG because it matches ARKANOID.CFG silently destroys that game's configuration the moment someone changes it. Aleph One likewise ships each scenario its own Readme.md. Exclude support files and game data from any dedupe; restrict it to rom extensions.

Which copy survives matters too. Prefer the one outside an Alternate Roms folder, then — for N64 — prefer .z64 over .n64 or .v64. When two such files are byte-identical the extension is only a label, and .z64 is the one every tool expects.

Empty rom directories are load-bearing

Deleting the empty system folders looks like obvious tidying. It is not: es_systems.cfg lists a system for every installed emulator, and several cores cover systems you may have no roms for — lr-genesis-plus-gx alone brings Master System and Game Gear. Remove the folders and every start logs Error - folder with path … is not a directory!, and adding a rom later has nowhere to go. Recreate any directory the config references:

grep -oE '<path>[^<]+</path>' /etc/emulationstation/es_systems.cfg \
  | sed 's|</*path>||g' | while read -r p; do [ -d "$p" ] || mkdir -p "$p"; done
Leave curated filenames alone. Names like 01. Sonic The Hedgehog 2 (World) (Rev A).md look like clutter until you count them: 104 of 104 on SNES, 83 of 83 on Game Boy. That consistency is a hand-built play order, and stripping the prefixes to match No-Intro convention destroys information that cannot be recovered from the files themselves.
Afterwards

Adding a second controller

A Logitech F310 in XInput mode needs no driver work — xpad claims it on plug-in and it appears as js1 beside the PiBoy's own pad. What it needs is two configuration files, and one of them is keyed on a value you cannot see.

Read the capability bitmaps instead of assuming a layout

/proc/bus/input/devices reports exactly what the pad exposes, which fixes the SDL indices without pressing a single button. KEY=7cdb000000000000 0 0 0 0 sets bits 48–62 of the word covering codes 256–319, i.e. codes 304, 305, 307, 308, 310, 311, 314, 315, 316, 317, 318 — the eleven standard buttons, in that order, so SDL numbers them 0–10. ABS=3003f gives axes 0–5 plus 16/17, meaning two sticks, two analog triggers and a real hat.

The triggers are axes, not buttons (ABS_Z/ABS_RZ), and the d-pad is a hat, not axes 6/7. Map them as input_l2_axis = "+2" and input_up_btn = "h0up"; treating either as an ordinary button leaves it working only at full travel, or not at all.

One more convention catches people: RetroPad is laid out SNES-style, so the pad's physical A (bottom) is RetroPad B, and physical B (right) is RetroPad A. Getting this backwards is the classic "my confirm button is wrong" complaint.

Derive the SDL GUID — and verify the derivation before trusting it

RetroArch matches a joypad profile by name, but EmulationStation matches by SDL GUID, so an es_input.cfg entry with a wrong GUID is simply ignored. The GUID is not random; it is eight little-endian 16-bit fields:

bus | crc16(name) | vendor | 0000 | product | 0000 | version | 0000

The name field is a CRC-16/ARC (reflected, polynomial 0xA001, init 0) over the device name. Everything else comes straight from /sys/class/input/inputN/id/.

Check the formula against a pad that already works before applying it to one that does not. The PiBoy's stored GUID is 15001176010000000100000000010000; computing CRC-16/ARC("PiBoy DMG Controller") gives 0x7611, matching bytes 2–3 exactly, and its bus/vendor/product/version line up with sysfs. Only once that reproduced a known-good value was it worth generating 030005ff6d0400001dc2000014400000 for the F310 — which EmulationStation then accepted, logging Added known joystick 'Logitech Gamepad F310'. Without that check the honest move is the built-in input wizard.

Note that running that wizard also regenerates the RetroArch profile from the ES configuration, overwriting a hand-written one. That is usually what you want; just do not be surprised by it.

The kernel delivers perfect input that SDL refuses to see

udev's input_id classifier tags the pad ID_INPUT_KEYBOARD — its Bluetooth HID descriptor carries a keyboard collection for the Xbox and Share buttons — and never sets ID_INPUT_JOYSTICK. SDL2's udev backend enumerates only devices carrying that tag, so EmulationStation and RetroArch cannot see the pad at all while the kernel delivers flawless BTN_GAMEPAD and EV_ABS events. Over USB the descriptor is clean and it works, which produces the diagnostic headline: the controller only works when plugged in.

Prove the kernel side before touching userspace. Reading /dev/input/eventN directly for 30 s while mashing buttons settles it in one step — 113 key events and 1739 axis events with correct codes means the fault is enumeration, not hardware. A rule scoped to bus and vendor plus either the device name or the product id corrects the single bad verdict without reimplementing the classifier. Match on the name as well as the id: Xbox pads change product id across firmware revisions.

The emulators were never slow

Measured

The charger, and what −250 mA actually meant

The battery reported Discharging at 100% with the charger plugged in, and amps never once read positive. That looks like a dead charge path. Chasing it produced three wrong theories before the measurement that settled it, and the sequence is worth recording because each wrong turn was individually reasonable.

The reading is real — prove that first

The decode is one line, and it is signed:

cur_val = (int)((signed char)data[8]) * 50;   // 50 mA per LSB

Load-testing it is the cheapest possible discriminator between a real sensor, a stuck value and a fixed sign. Four cores at 2000 MHz moved it from -250 to -350 and back — responsive, therefore real. But 100 mA is far too small a delta for a load that should add 800–900 mA, which is what launched the wrong theory: that the *50 scale factor was off by roughly 8×.

The decisive test is removing the charger, not adding load. Unplugged, the same reading went to -1650 mA and the pack voltage began falling — 6.7 W at 4.05 V, exactly right for an overclocked Pi 4 with a panel and fan. The scale was never wrong. Plugged in, the charger simply carries nearly all of the load and the pack contributes a trickle near the noise floor. At 50 mA per LSB, −250 mA is five counts from zero, and building a theory on it was over-reading the instrument.

A voltage-derived gauge sags under load — that is not discharge

On battery the pack read 4045 mV and "89%". Reconnecting the charger snapped it back to 4200 mV and "100%" within seconds. Nothing charges 11% in five seconds: the gauge is derived from terminal voltage, and terminal voltage sags under a 1.65 A draw and rebounds the moment the load moves to the charger. The pack was near-full throughout. Expect any voltage-derived capacity figure on a handheld to read low while playing and jump when plugged in.

So why is the current always negative?

Because the pack has been full the entire time. A full pack on a terminated charger sits at zero, and zero on this sensor dithers between 0 and -50. Positive current would require a genuinely depleted pack, which two minutes of unplugged testing never produced — it only produced sag.

Input

The parts that fail silently

Every input problem found on this build shared a shape: nothing logged, nothing errored, the control simply did nothing. Collected here because the symptom gives no clue which layer is at fault.

A dead controller that is really a restored savestate

Donkey Kong 64 began launching mid-session and accepting no input — not from the built-in pad, not from a Bluetooth controller. That is the exact signature of dead input hardware, and neither was at fault. RetroArch was restoring a .state.auto that had been written while the game was already wedged, so every launch faithfully reproduced a hung session.

The two settings responsible belong to neither RetroPie nor Experimental Pi. RetroArch's own skeleton documents the default, and with auto-save off no .state.auto is ever written, so auto-load has nothing to restore and the failure cannot occur:

# RetroArch's documented defaults
# savestate_auto_save = false
# savestate_auto_load = true
Enabling auto-save makes one bad session permanent. A state is written on every exit, including exits from a game that had already hung, and auto-load then restores it on every launch thereafter. The symptom is indistinguishable from broken input, which sends you hunting the controller, the driver and the pairing before the emulator. Recovery is deleting <rom>.state.auto beside the ROM. Never delete .srm — that is the real in-game battery save and is unrelated.

When a setting has no obvious author, date it rather than assume it. Timestamped copies of retroarch.cfg placed these two lines in a specific window, and they sat above the file's own ## Skeleton config file for RetroArch header, which marks them as prepended rather than native. grep -rn savestate_auto across RetroPie-Setup/scriptmodules and every shipped retroarch.cfg.rp-dist returned nothing, ruling out both RetroPie and the original image — which left this build's own history as the source.

The launch menu ignores the gamepad — joy2key was never installed

RetroPie's runcommand menu is a dialog box that reads only the keyboard. A helper called joy2key bridges the pad to it, and runcommand.sh gives up without a word when it is missing:

function start_joy2key() {
    [[ "$DISABLE_JOYSTICK" -eq 1 || ! -f "$JOY2KEY" ]] && return

A source-built RetroPie installs emulator cores but not this, so the d-pad and buttons do nothing in the one menu you cannot avoid. sudo ./retropie_packages.sh joy2key fixes it. Note the default mapping sends Space on button 1 and Esc on button 2, so on a Game Boy-style face X cancels rather than B; swapping those two codes is a one-line change to the wrapper.

Console emulators have no way out, and the only exit is a power cycle

RetroArch exits on Start+Select. Emulators launched with the CON: prefix — frotz and anything else console-based — never see a RetroArch hotkey, so on a device with no keyboard attached the only remaining exit is cutting power. On hardware where every unclean shutdown risks the filesystem, that is the one exit path worth engineering away.

A universal escape hatch collides with normal play unless it is held. The obvious implementation — watch for both buttons, kill the emulator — is wrong, because most ports run standalone rather than under RetroArch, and in Duke Nukem or Quake III Start is an ordinary menu button. An instant trigger kills the game mid-play. Requiring a two-second hold cannot collide with in-game use. Guard it further so it fires only while runcommand.sh is running (never on the ES menu) and only when RetroArch is absent (which keeps its own hotkey). Watch evdev codes rather than joystick indices and it works for every pad without per-device configuration.
Scanning /dev/input once at startup is not enough. A Bluetooth pad reconnects asynchronously and slowly, so on most boots it arrives well after the daemon has already scanned — and the escape hatch silently does not cover it. Watch the directory with inotify rather than rescanning on a timer: the daemon reconciles exactly when a device appears or leaves, and idle cost stays at zero wakeups, which a polling rescan would give away. Include IN_ATTRIB, not only IN_CREATE — udev chowns the node to root:input after creating it, so a daemon running as the console user loses that race and needs the second event. Reconcile incrementally: closing and reopening every fd discards the recorded button state, and any pad with buttons down at that instant latches the combo for the process lifetime.

Restarting EmulationStation over SSH breaks every emulator at once

runcommand launches each emulator as eval "$COMMAND" </dev/tty. A process with no controlling terminal cannot open /dev/tty, so the command never runs: the console flashes and EmulationStation returns, for every system, with nothing logged anywhere.

ps -o pid,ppid,tty,cmd -p $(pgrep -f emulationstation)
  4468   1   ?   /bin/bash /usr/bin/emulationstation      # TT "?" = no terminal
nohup emulationstation & from an SSH session is the trap. It reparents to systemd with no TTY, while the autologin shell on tty1 sits idle having lost the process it started. Restore the login, not the program: sudo systemctl restart getty@tty1, then confirm ps -o tty= reports tty1 rather than ?. Diagnosing this from the emulator side is hopeless — the identical command launched over SSH succeeds every time, because that session has a terminal.

The last core installed claims the default

Installing lr-fbneo for arcade silently made it the default for NES as well, because it registers an nes subsystem. Every NES game then failed with Romset is Unknown — FBNeo matches by romset name and a file called 01. Super Mario Bros. 3 (USA).nes matches nothing. Three other systems had no default at all. Audit after any batch install:

for s in /opt/retropie/configs/*/; do
  grep -H '^default' "$s/emulators.cfg" 2>/dev/null
done

ioquake3 ships with the joystick switched off

Two separate faults, and fixing only the first leaves the pad still broken. seta in_joystick "0" is the shipping default, so nothing registers. Enable it and the look axes are still wrong: ioquake3 defaults yaw/pitch to axes 2 and 3, but on an XInput pad axis 2 is the left trigger. The right stick is axes 3 and 4.

Put it in autoexec.cfg, not q3config.cfg. Quake III rewrites q3config.cfg on every quit, silently reverting your edits. autoexec.cfg runs afterwards and is never touched. Modern engines — eduke32, Aleph One — use the SDL_GameController API instead and auto-map any recognised pad with no configuration at all; ioquake3 predates that abstraction.

One pad at a time, incidentally: in_joystickNo selects a single device, and single-player engines open controller index 0. On a handheld with one stick, bind the stick to look and the d-pad to movement — digital strafing is merely old-fashioned, digital aiming is unplayable. Point unused axis cvars at axes that exist and zero their scale, or the engine logs bad axis continuously.

Remap per core, never globally

N64 needed its face buttons swapped and nothing else did. A per-core remap in the system's input_remapping_directory touches exactly one emulator:

# /opt/retropie/configs/n64/Mupen64Plus-Next/Mupen64Plus-Next.rmp
# RetroPad ids: 0=B 1=Y 2=Select 3=Start 4..7=dpad 8=A 9=X 10=L 11=R
input_player1_btn_a = "0"
input_player1_btn_b = "8"

The filename must match the core's internal corename (Mupen64Plus-Next), not the RetroPie module name (lr-mupen64plus-next). Note also that RetroArch overwrites this file if you save a remap from the in-game menu.

Not every odd mapping is a bug. Genesis Plus GX maps Genesis A/B/C onto RetroPad Y/B/A by documented default. It looks wrong on a diamond face and is not — the Genesis pad has three buttons in a row and a four-button diamond cannot reproduce that. Remapping it to "fix" the appearance makes the device disagree with every guide written for it.

Translating Experimental Pi's display config

The original ran legacy fkms, where timings were a firmware-parsed string. Under full KMS the same numbers become device-tree properties. This mapping is the core of the port.

The output format decodes by nibble: low nibble 6 is 18-bit RGB666 CFG2, the next nibble 1 is RGB ordering. CFG2 is the pad-high pin layout, which is why Experimental Pi also carried gpio=0-9,12-17,20-25=a2. Under KMS the overlay handles that muxing itself. Confirm the result in the live tree — bus-format should read 0x1015, not the default 0x1009:

od -An -tx4 --endian=big /proc/device-tree/panel/*bus*format*
cat /sys/class/drm/card0-DPI-1/modes

70 Hz is a choice, and on a Pi 4 it is the wrong one

The timings above are Experimental Pi's shipped default, and they yield 70.06 Hz — 32000000 / (880 × 519). That is not an artefact of the port. Their config carries a commented menu of alternatives from 60 to 85 Hz and annotates one line #alternate 60hz setting for Pi4 only. On this board that line is their own recommendation, validated on this panel, and preferable to a pixel clock derived from first principles:

dpi_timings=640 1 56 4 42 480 1 16 4 12 0 0 0 60 0 22800000 1
        ↓
dtparam=clock-frequency=22800000
dtparam=hactive=640,hfp=56,hsync=4,hbp=42
dtparam=vactive=480,vfp=16,vsync=4,vbp=12

742 × 512 at 22.8 MHz is 60.0152 Hz. Confirm it in a verbose RetroArch log rather than modes, which reports resolution only:

[DRM]: Mode 0: (640x480) 640 x 480, 60.015163 Hz
A non-integer refresh ratio reads as slowness, not as stutter. At 70.06 Hz against 60 fps content, about ten frames per second are displayed twice: most last 14.3 ms, ten last 28.6 ms. Nothing is dropped — so it does not look like dropped frames — but continuous-motion games feel heavy, and the natural way to describe that is “running at 80–90% speed”. Profiling the emulator will never find it, because the emulator is not the problem.

What the driver exposes

Once loaded, /sys/kernel/xpi_gamecon/ carries the board's telemetry and controls. All nodes are 0660 root:root. Live, changing values here are also the quickest proof the GPIO protocol is talking.

The fan scale catches people out. Experimental Pi's osd.cfg lists its bands as percentages, so a configured 20 % appears here as 51. Anything that treats the node as 0–100 will run the fan at roughly 40 % of what you asked for.

Three things that were not bugs

Worth recording, because each looks like an obvious defect and none needs fixing.

The driver derives the peripheral base by reading the /soc ranges property by u32 index — which ought to break under a 64-bit kernel's two-cell parent addresses. It doesn't. The author wrote a fallback that reads index 1, sees the high word 0x00000000, and drops to index 2 for 0xfe000000. It resolves correctly on both architectures.

Similarly, the panel needed no sync-polarity inversion. Experimental Pi's timings carried h_sync_polarity=1 and v_sync_polarity=1, which map onto the overlay's defaults; adding hsync-invert/vsync-invert would have broken a working display.

The red LED ignores you, and should

Writes to green work and are true PWM brightness — 15 is visibly dimmer than 100, so pulsing and breathing patterns are available. Writes to red do nothing at any value, alone or combined, even though the element clearly lights on its own. That reads as a broken output. It is not.

The driver does transmit the value. The outbound protocol round-robins four settings, one per packet, selected by a high byte:

data[GC_LENGTH+0] = 0xC0 | (index & 0x3);      // 0xC2 = red, 0xC3 = green
data[GC_LENGTH+1] = values.data[index & 0x3];  // flags, fan, red, green

Red goes out as selector 0xC2 every fourth packet and the XPi discards it, because that channel is firmware-owned. Experimental Pi documents a single bi-colour LED whose red element is a battery gauge: green at 25% or above, then yellow, then a second yellow, then red as the pack drains. It also lights with no battery fitted, and flashes a green-then-red diagnostic when the Pi is not communicating at all — behaviour that cannot involve software running on the Pi.

Green is yours; red is the firmware's. Register green as a leds classdev and let the kernel animate it. Expose red read-only — a writable node that silently does nothing is worse than no node, and this one consumed two separate investigations. Note also that low-battery indication in a userland daemon duplicates what the firmware already signals on the same LED.
A caution on method. Brief four-to-five second sweeps produced results that contradicted each other and sent us chasing a non-existent brightness-scale bug. Hold each state until it is confirmed by eye. Better still, check whether Experimental Pi documented the behaviour before measuring at all — the answer here sat in a public troubleshooting page the whole time.
Open

What is not solved

Plugging in USB changes who player 1 is

With correct autoconfig profiles for both of the Elite's identities nothing is ever dead — but the USB pad sorts ahead of the built-in controls in libudev order, so attaching the cable moves the Elite to port 1 and the PiBoy's own buttons to port 2. Connecting a controller merely to charge it mid-game therefore shifts control.

The obvious remedy, pinning input_player1_joypad_index, is worse than the problem: indices are positional, so the pin addresses nothing the moment that pad disappears and the built-in controls go dead. RetroArch offers no name-based port binding. The practical options are charging from a source other than the Pi, or swapping the device index for the session from the in-game menu. Left unresolved deliberately — a dynamic assignment that is occasionally surprising beats a static one that is occasionally fatal.

Replacing Experimental Pi's OSD — mostly a solved problem

The osd binary is a 32-bit armhf ELF linking /opt/vc/lib/libbcm_host.so, libvchiq_arm.so and libvcos.so — the legacy Broadcom VideoCore userland. That stack does not exist on 64-bit Raspberry Pi OS, dispmanx does not work under full KMS anyway, and multiarch cannot save it because those libraries were never ported. It will not run again.

But it turns out it was doing four jobs, and only one of them was graphics:

Exposing the battery through the kernel's standard interface is a genuine improvement on what Experimental Pi shipped: instead of a proprietary sysfs tree only their binary understood, every tool on the system can read it.

Drawing over a running game without dispmanx

The obstacle is not drawing, it is DRM master exclusivity. The DPI CRTC has plenty of free overlay planes (plane-1 is the primary; plane-0, plane-2…9 sit unused), but only one process may hold DRM master on a CRTC, and EmulationStation and RetroArch each take it via SDL's KMSDRM backend. An independent overlay daemon cannot commit to those planes while a game owns the display. dispmanx let Experimental Pi sidestep exactly this.

The cheap way through is to ask the app that does own the display to draw for you. RetroArch ships a UDP command interface:

# retroarch.cfg
network_cmd_enable = "true"
network_cmd_port   = "55355"

# Widgets render every SHOW_MSG as its own card, so a wheel sweep stacks
# dozens of them. Plain OSD draws one line that replaces itself - which is
# what a volume slider wants. Note this is global: it also turns off the
# achievement and fast-forward widgets.
menu_enable_widgets = "false"

# push a message onto the running game — no netcat needed
exec 3<>/dev/udp/127.0.0.1/55355 && printf "SHOW_MSG %s" "Volume: 45%" >&3

Ownership is split by which daemon owns the event. piboy-vold draws the volume slider, because it is the process holding the wheel's evdev fd; it renders the position as Volume [========--------] 50%. piboy-fand fires the same mechanism when the battery crosses 25 / 15 / 10 / 5% while discharging — each threshold once, re-arming above 30%. A low-battery alert that finds you mid-game is arguably more useful than Experimental Pi's static meter, which you had to look at.

For the menu itself, menu_battery_level_enable shows charge in RGUI's top-right — but only alongside menu_timedate_enable, since in RGUI they share one status line. And note RetroPie's EmulationStation has no status display of any kind: no clock, no battery, nothing in the binary to enable.

What a real status bar would take

A permanent strip over both the frontend and games means running a Wayland compositor (labwc, cage) as DRM master, with ES and RetroArch as clients and a layer-shell bar (waybar, yambar) above them. That is the honest modern equivalent of dispmanx. The blocker is that RetroPie builds RetroArch without Wayland:

retroarch --features
  Wayland  - Wayland input/video drivers: no
  KMS      - Video context driver: yes

So it is a compositor install, a full RetroArch rebuild from source with Wayland enabled, reworked input routing, and a compositing cost on a 640×480 panel where a 20 px bar is 4% of the screen. Treat it as its own project, with a working image backed up first.

Back up the binary regardless. It ships unstripped with debug symbols, making it the best surviving documentation of the XPi protocol — worth having if anyone ever wants to chase the red LED channel or the drawn overlay.

Measured: leave the bit-bang clock alone

The driver busy-spins in udelay() to clock its 2-wire protocol, and the half-period is a compile-time constant. Arithmetic suggests it costs a fifth of a core permanently, which makes lowering it look like the biggest available win. Make it a writable module parameter, add cumulative CRC counters (Experimental Pi's lastgood/lasterror are consecutive-run counters and cannot express a rate), and sweep it:

BITRATE  GOOD/s  CRC/s  ERR_RATE
7        77      0      0.000%    Experimental Pi's default
6        77      0      0.000%
5        76      0      0.000%    fastest error-free
4        77      0      0.129%    errors begin
3        76      0      0.774%
2         0     78      100.00%   protocol collapses

So Experimental Pi's 7 has about two steps of margin and was not arbitrary. But no measurement supported changing it, and three separate attempts failed for three different reasons worth knowing:

  • softirq accounting reads zero — the kernel does not attribute udelay() busy-wait in a timer callback to softirq time.
  • A throughput benchmark shows nothing, even at bitrate 20 (nearly 3× the spin). The timer spins on one core while a single-threaded workload runs on another of four.
  • Discharge current cannot resolve it — the XPi decodes current as (signed char) * 50, so the sensor is quantised to 50 mA. Every amps reading being a round multiple of 50 is this, not coincidence.

The cost may well be real and simply invisible to every instrument on the device. Trading two steps of protocol margin — on the link carrying controls, battery telemetry and the power switch — for an unmeasurable saving is a bad bargain. A bench supply with a current meter on the rail would settle it in minutes; until then, leave it at 7.

Register with the kernel's subsystems, not a private sysfs tree

Experimental Pi exposed everything through one bespoke kobject — /sys/kernel/xpi_gamecon/{fan,green,volume,battery,…} — which only their binary understood. Four small registrations put the same hardware behind interfaces the rest of Linux already speaks, and each one deletes userspace code rather than adding it.

RegistrationAppears asWhat it removes
led_classdev_register()/sys/class/leds/piboy:green:statusAll LED animation. Kernel triggers blink and fade

The compounding effect is the part worth noticing: registering the battery auto-creates LED triggers. With both a leds class device and a power_supply present, the kernel offers xpi-battery-charging, xpi-battery-full and xpi-battery-charging-blink-full-solid among its 38 triggers. The "gentle charging breathe" that started life as a bash array indexed off a tick counter is now one echo of a trigger name, animated by the kernel.

Use a separate input device for the wheel rather than another axis on the gamepad — an extra axis would show up to SDL as a game control and confuse emulator input mapping.

A fan watchdog beats a cooling device here. Binding a thermal_cooling_device to trip points is the textbook answer, but it needs an OF node — and this module is a bare module_init that ioremaps a hand-probed peripheral base, so it would first have to become a platform driver. The non-OF binding path was reworked and de-exported around 6.8, so it will not span 5.10–6.18 either. Far cheaper: have the driver hold a safe floor whenever nobody has written pwm1 for ~30 s. That covers the boot gap, the crash gap and the systemctl stop gap in about fifteen lines, and belongs with pwm1_enable so the semantics are standard hwmon rather than invented.
Then make the governor feed it. A daemon that writes pwm1 only when the duty changes will be overridden by that watchdog the moment the temperature is stable for 30 s — the fan silently jumps to the floor and stays there. Write every cycle; the sysfs write is a shell builtin and costs nothing. Both halves are correct alone and wrong together, and a single sample taken within 30 s of a write looks perfect.
Publishing order matters. If a "do we have valid data yet" flag is latched before the values it guards are stored, a reader on another core can observe the flag set while the data is still zero — reporting a battery that is present at 0%, which is exactly what such a guard exists to prevent. Store the values, smp_wmb(), then publish the flag with WRITE_ONCE(); pair it with READ_ONCE() and smp_rmb() in the property getter. The Pi 4 is SMP and the getter runs from a userspace read on another core.

Consume the input device, or do not create it

Registering ABS_VOLUME and then still polling the private sysfs node is the worst of both: the kernel's fuzz filter never applies to that reader, so the deadband ends up implemented twice in two languages that can drift. Move volume to a small evdev consumer that blocks on the fd — no polling, no per-change amixer fork, and it is the only thing that lets the rest of the daemon collapse to a plain multi-second loop.

Bash cannot do this (input events are binary struct input_event records), so it is a short C program. Two things bite there:

  • glibc's signal() sets SA_RESTART, so a blocking read() restarts after the handler and the process ignores SIGTERM entirely — it dies only to SIGKILL. Use sigaction() with sa_flags = 0.
  • The dB API can set mute instead of a level. This control reports its dB minimum as the mute sentinel, so interpolating from it lands below the audible floor. Use the raw volume range, which is already linear in dB on this card and therefore already perceptually linear.
Mute above zero, not at it. input_defuzz_abs_event() drops changes smaller than fuzz/2, so with any useful fuzz the final wheel 1 → 0 step never reaches userspace: volume rests at the floor and stays quietly audible forever. Treat anything at or below a small threshold as mute, and toggle the element's pswitch as well — minimum volume is not necessarily silence.

Two unexplained shutdowns — with a strong candidate

The unit powered itself off twice in one session: full battery, ~55 °C, no thermal or under-voltage flags, and the second with a known-good module during nothing more taxing than a systemd-journald restart.

The likeliest explanation only emerged later, from the power-switch work above: a shutdown request nobody answers looks exactly like a spontaneous power-off. The XPi asserts the bit, waits ~31 seconds, and drops the rail — no flags, no warning, nothing in a volatile journal. A switch that is marginal, or simply nudged, produces precisely the observed signature. Not proven for those two events, but it is a real mechanism that was demonstrably live on this hardware for the whole session.

Neither event left evidence, because journald was volatile the entire time — including through several attempts to enable persistence that silently did nothing (see the traps). Fix that first; it is the difference between diagnosing the next one and guessing again.

Note also that an unexpected power-off can leave dpkg mid-transaction, after which every package operation fails until sudo dpkg --configure -a is run. Two RetroPie packages failed at their dependency step for exactly this reason, which reads as a build error and is not one.

Positive charge current has never been observed

Everything about the charge path checks out — VBus detection flips cleanly on unplug, the current reading tracks load and source correctly, and the pack demonstrably holds charge. But amps has never once read above zero, because the pack has been full or near-full every time it was measured. Two minutes on battery produces voltage sag, not depletion. Settling it needs a genuine 20–30 minute discharge followed by a plug-in, and until then it is unproven whether the sensor reports charge current at all or only discharge.

NeoGeo is blocked by content, not by software

The core is installed and configured; 328 romsets are present. Neither matters. There is no neogeo.zip BIOS anywhere on the card — not as a file, not inside any of the 328 archives — and FBNeo starts no NeoGeo title without it. The backup restored from Experimental Pi's own card did not contain one either, which means NeoGeo never worked on the original machine. Separately, 295 of the 328 archives were renamed to friendly titles (3countbout.zip holding 043-*.rom, which FBNeo knows as 3countb), and FBNeo identifies romsets by filename. The cart number inside each archive is a reliable key for renaming them back, but the BIOS is the hard blocker.

Soft reboot does not come back

systemctl reboot takes the unit down and it stays down — the XPi appears to cut power on halt without re-asserting it, so the physical switch has to be flipped. Worth knowing before you issue a remote reboot on a device sitting in another room, and worth designing around if you script anything.

The rail really is low. avoid_warnings=2 restores full speed by suppressing the firmware's reaction, not by fixing the voltage — the XPi's boost output still dips below what the Pi expects. Experimental Pi shipped it this way for years, so it is evidently survivable, but you are running the SoC through those dips. Treat clean shutdowns as mandatory and keep backups current.

Worked out against a PiBoy DMG with a Pi 4B rev 1.4, Raspberry Pi OS Trixie (2026-06-18, arm64 Lite), kernel 6.18.34. The build was done on a separate card while the original 1 TB RetroPie card was left untouched — an approach worth copying. That original was then imaged, the image verified against its checksums, and the finished system migrated onto it; the build card is now the rollback.
